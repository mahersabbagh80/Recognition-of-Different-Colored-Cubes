Your goal: explain why thousands of candidate predictions can describe only a few cubes, and undo the image preparation for one box. Take lesson 9 first.

1. Where can 8,400 candidates come from?

A feature map is a grid of learned responses to image patterns. A detection head turns those responses into proposed boxes and class scores. A candidate is one proposal; it need not describe a real object. Multiple proposals may describe the same cube.

Consider a detector with three grids and one candidate per position. For a 640 × 640 input, grids of 80 × 80, 40 × 40 and 20 × 20 contribute 6,400 + 1,600 + 400 = 8,400 positions. Their stride is the spacing between neighboring grid positions measured in input pixels: 8, 16 and 32 respectively. This spacing is not a predicted box's width. A fine grid offers denser positions; it does not guarantee finding a small cube.

The project decoder documents [1, 7, 8400]: one image, seven values per candidate, and 8,400 candidates. That shape is consistent with the example above; shape alone does not prove the architecture of a newly exported model. Check its actual graph and output contract before reusing this decoder.

2. Read one candidate, not the whole tensor

A tensor here is a numeric array; its axes identify what each dimension counts. Removing the one-image axis and transposing (swapping the two remaining axes) gives [8400, 7], one row per candidate. The seven fields are center x, center y, width, height, then blue, green and red scores. This contract has no extra objectness field.

For scores [0.08, 0.91, 0.14], max returns the largest value, 0.91. argmax returns its zero-based position, 1, meaning green under this class order. These scores need not sum to one. A confidence mask keeps rows whose chosen score reaches the cutoff. Choosing a class alone has removed no rows.

Follow the exact boundary in the project decoder:

pred = out[0].transpose()
boxes = pred[:, :4]
scores = pred[:, 4:]
cls_conf = scores.max(axis=1)
cls_id = scores.argmax(axis=1)

3. Undo the image preparation

Letterboxing scales an image equally in both directions, then adds border pixels called padding to fit a canvas. A coordinate frame identifies which image a position belongs to. A rectangle drawn in the model canvas cannot be used directly on the original camera frame.

Take a 1280 × 720 camera frame and a 640 × 640 model canvas. The scale ratio r = 0.5 produces a 640 × 360 image. The top padding is 140 pixels; left padding is zero. Reverse the operations in reverse order: x_camera = (x_model − pad_left) / r and y_camera = (y_model − pad_top) / r.

A model box with corners (270, 280, 370, 360) therefore becomes (540, 280, 740, 440) on the camera image. Subtracting padding without dividing by the ratio would leave the box half-sized.
