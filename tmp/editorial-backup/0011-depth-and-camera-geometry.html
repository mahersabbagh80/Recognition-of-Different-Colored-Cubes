Depth and camera geometry

A pixel gives a viewing direction. To estimate a physical point, the camera model, depth convention, alignment, and coordinate frame must agree.

1. A pixel is a direction

A pixel is one sampled location in an image. 2-D means two dimensions, here horizontal and vertical image position. 3-D means three spatial coordinates. A viewing ray is the half-line that starts at the camera's optical centre, the ideal projection centre of the lens, and passes through one pixel. The optical axis is the camera's central forward reference direction. Without depth, every possible object on that ray looks like a candidate point.

Perspective projection is the camera operation that maps a 3-D point to a 2-D pixel. It loses scale: a small nearby cube and a large distant cube can occupy similar image areas. A bounding box, which is a rectangle around an image object, therefore does not by itself give physical size or distance.

Central mental modelPixel + camera calibration = a ray. Pixel + valid depth + calibration = a 3-D point in a named camera frame. A camera frame is a coordinate system attached to the camera; it is not automatically the robot's coordinate system.

2. Calibration, registration, and the pinhole equations

Camera calibration estimates parameters that describe a camera. Intrinsics are internal image parameters: fx and fy are focal lengths measured in pixels. A focal length is an image-scale factor: larger focal length makes the same off-axis point appear farther from the principal point. cx and cy are the principal point, the pixel where the optical axis meets the image. Extrinsics describe the rigid relationship between two coordinate frames: a rotation changes orientation and a translation changes origin. Intuitively, extrinsics tell us how the camera is mounted relative to the robot.

The common pinhole model approximates the camera as one projection centre with straight rays. Lens distortion is the real lens's bending or warping of ideal straight-ray positions. A rectified image has that distortion corrected so corresponding pixels follow the pinhole model. The equations below are useful under that rectified pinhole assumption, with a depth value that really is optical Z.

X = (u − cx) × Z / fx
Y = (v − cy) × Z / fy
Z = optical-axis depth

Back-projection means reversing the image projection to recover a 3-D point from a pixel and depth. Here u and v are the horizontal and vertical pixel coordinates. X, Y, and Z are the point's coordinates in the camera frame. The symbol = means “is equal to”; − means subtraction; × means multiplication; and / means division. A resolution is an image's width and height in pixels. The abbreviation px means pixel. Calibration parameters belong to a particular camera stream and resolution, so a crop or resize needs matching coordinate handling.

  • Worked example: u=420, cx=320, fx=500 px, Z=1 m.
  • Substitute: X=(420−320)×1/500=0.2 m.
  • Interpret: the point is 0.2 m to the camera-frame right of the optical axis.

Registration is a separate job. It transforms or resamples one sensor's image so a pixel in the RGB grid refers to the same physical ray as the depth sample. Calibration can be correct while registration is wrong. Equal width and height also do not prove physical alignment: two 640×480 images can still come from different viewpoints or crops.

3. Depth conventions, units, and robust samples

Optical Z-depth is the distance measured along the camera's optical Z axis. It is not the ray range, the straight-line distance from the camera centre to the point. The distinction matters away from the image centre. ROS means Robot Operating System; REP means ROS Enhancement Proposal. REP 118's canonical ROS representation uses a 32-bit floating-point image whose depth is in metres. The SI (International System of Units) length unit is the metre. A common raw representation uses uint16, an unsigned 16-bit integer, in millimetres; in that convention 0 means invalid. A floating-point NaN (“not a number”) is another invalid marker. Always read the message encoding and sensor documentation before converting units.

A box contains foreground, the visible target surface, as well as floor, edges, and missing samples. A valid sample passes the sensor's validity rules. A median is the middle value after sorting valid samples. With an even count, average the two middle values: for [1048, 1050, 1051, 1052], the median is (1050+1051)/2 = 1050.5. A percentile is a chosen point in that sorted distribution; p90 means the 90th percentile. In this lesson, the nearest-rank p90 is the value at rank ceil(0.90 × n): ceil means round upward, and n is the count of valid samples. A rank counts positions starting at 1, so rank 1 selects the smallest valid reading. For five readings, ceil(0.90×5) = ceil(4.5) = 5: select the fifth value. With no valid readings, neither statistic can be calculated. For the illustrative millimetre values [1048, 1050, 1051, 1052, 1062, 0], discard the invalid zero: the median is 1051 mm, while the nearest-rank p90 is 1062 mm. The median describes a typical sample; p90 exposes a high-depth tail. If too few values are valid, report low evidence instead of pretending the statistic is trustworthy.

4. Camera axes are not robot axes

REP 103 uses an optical camera frame with x right, y down, and z forward. A typical robot body frame uses x forward, y left, and z up. A frame is only a naming convention until its axes and origin are declared. To use a camera point for a robot, apply the calibrated extrinsic rotation and translation; this is a coordinate conversion, not a new detection.

“Nearer than the floor” is not universally the same as “taller than the floor.” With a level, overhead-like view, a raised cube often has lower optical Z than the floor behind it. With an oblique (slanted) camera, the floor itself has changing Z across the image. A sound height test first models the floor plane, an ideal flat surface in 3-D, then measures a point's distance from that plane. A single global floor depth is only an assumption that must be checked against the mounting geometry.

A predicate is a true-or-false comparison. A margin is a required gap beyond a reference value. For example, with floor depth 1500 mm and margin 20 mm, “nearer by more than the margin” means surface depth < 1500−20. The symbol < means less than. A surface at 1400 mm passes because 1400 < 1480. This compares optical depths; it does not measure height above a slanted floor.

Applied to our cubes

Repository observationThe current node uses an approximate RGB/depth timestamp synchronizer, a component that pairs messages by nearby timestamps. It converts RGB with cv_bridge, a ROS image-message conversion package; requests depth with passthrough encoding, which preserves the source array's numeric representation; and returns early unless depth is a 2-D uint16 array. It reads intrinsics from the RGB CameraInfo message, the ROS message carrying camera calibration metadata, when available, with configured fallbacks.

Confirmed code behaviorgeometry_filter.py collects valid millimetre samples, estimates an annulus, meaning a ring around the box, for a floor median, and computes a raised mask, a boolean selection grid, with depth > floor_mm + raised_mm. Under ordinary optical Z, that sign selects farther points, not nearer raised surfaces. It is a code fact and a teaching example; it does not validate the hardware mounting, registration, or floor interpretation. When statistics are invalid or below the minimum count, decide returns KEEP, meaning allow this candidate to pass, with a low-quality reason, so sparse depth is visible rather than silently filtered.
